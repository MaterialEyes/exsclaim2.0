      The nitrate-to-ammonia electrochemical reduction reaction (NO3RR) driven by renewable energy provides a sustainable route for simultaneous wastewater treatment and green ammonia synthesis. Nevertheless, the NO3RR involves a complex multi-step proton-coupled electron transfer process, and the imbalance between proton supply and electron transfer kinetics often leads to poor selectivity toward NH3 and severe competition from the parasitic hydrogen evolution reaction (HER). Herein, a series of Ni doped Cu2O (Ni/Cu2O) catalysts with a well-defined cubic morphology are developed via a facile one-pot reduction strategy to overcome these limitations. Theoretical calculations demonstrate that Ni incorporation effectively modulates the electronic structure of Cu2O, facilitating water dissociation to generate sufficient surface *H species and thereby accelerating the hydrogenation of nitrogen-containing intermediates. Meanwhile, enhanced orbital interactions between the catalyst surface and *NO intermediates promote the *NO2 → *NO conversion, leading to improved reaction kinetics and selectivity. The optimized Ni/Cu2O catalyst with enriched surface hydroxyl/water chemisorb amount and abundant catalytic active sites achieves a high NH3 faradaic efficiency (FE) of 91.11 ± 3.43% and an NH3 yield rate of 7.81 ± 0.53 mg h−1 mgcat−1 at −0.9 V vs. RHE and reduced charge-transfer resistance, outperforming pristine Cu2O. This work highlights the synergistic roles of facet engineering and electronic modulation, providing a rational framework for the design of high-performance, earth-abundant electrocatalysts for sustainable nitrate-to-ammonia conversion.

      1 Introduction

      Ammonia (NH3) is one of the most widely produced chemicals, playing a vital role in both industrial manufacturing and agriculture.1  More recently, it has attracted increasing attention as a promising carbon-free energy carrier, particularly as a hydrogen storage medium and a potential fuel for transportation.2–4  Conventionally, NH3 is synthesized via the Haber–Bosch process, an energy-demanding method that operates at elevated temperatures and pressures, contributing significantly to global greenhouse gas emissions.5,6  As a greener alternative, electrochemical nitrate-to-ammonia conversion has emerged as a sustainable strategy, especially because it can utilize nitrate-contaminated wastewater as a feedstock.7,8  This approach not only removes harmful pollutants but also enables ammonia production under ambient conditions, aligning well with the principles of green chemistry and the artificial nitrogen cycle. Nevertheless, NO3RR is intrinsically challenging, as it involves a complicated eight-electron and nine-proton transfer pathway.9  This complexity often leads to poor selectivity due to the weak adsorption of nitrogen-containing intermediates on catalyst surfaces.10  Moreover, inadequate energy for water dissociation can cause the buildup of reaction intermediates, whereas excessive activation may favor the parasitic HER.11,12  Consequently, designing efficient NO3RR electrocatalysts requires a careful balance between appropriate intermediate adsorption strength and effective hydrogen activation capability.

      Copper (Cu)-based catalysts have been widely investigated due to their strong electrostatic affinity toward NO3− and favorable orbital interactions, which facilitate effective adsorption and activation of nitrate species.13–15  However, these catalysts typically exhibit weak interaction with *NO2 intermediates and limited hydrogenation ability.16,17  As a result, the reaction pathway can be hindered, leading to the accumulation of NO2− as a byproduct and consequently reducing the efficiency of NH3 formation. To overcome these drawbacks, the construction of dual-active sites by integrating Cu with noble metals (such as Ru, Rh, Pt, and Pd) has been extensively explored.18–20  This strategy can synergistically promote both hydrogenation and deoxygenation steps. Nevertheless, the high cost and limited availability of noble metals motivate the development of catalysts based on earth-abundant transition metals. Previous studies have demonstrated that nickel (Ni) is an effective catalyst for the HER.21,22  Therefore, modifying Cu-based catalysts with Ni is considered a promising approach to enhance NO3RR performance. In such systems, Cu sites could preferentially adsorb *NO3 and facilitate its conversion to *NO2, while Ni sites contribute to generating *H species via water dissociation, thereby promoting the hydrogenation of nitrogen-containing intermediates.23–25  This cooperative mechanism can accelerate the overall reaction kinetics and improve the selectivity toward ammonia production. Furthermore, if water dissociation is not sufficiently activated, reaction intermediates may accumulate on the surface, whereas overly facile dissociation can favor the parasitic HER.

      In this work, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were first conducted to assess the catalytic performance and elucidate the reaction mechanism of Ni-doped Cu2O (111) and (001) surfaces for NO3−-to-NH3 conversion by analyzing the energetics along the reaction pathway. The computational results indicate that electronic modulation induced by Ni introduction could enhance the surface availability of *H species, thereby facilitating the hydrogenation of nitrogen-containing intermediates. In addition, improved orbital interactions between the catalyst surface and *NO provide a clear electronic explanation for the promoted *NO2 → *NO transformation and the subsequent hydrogenation steps on Ni/Cu2O(111) and Ni/Cu2O(001). These effects collectively demonstrate that Ni substitution synergistically enhances the overall NO3− reduction reaction toward NH3 formation, regardless of the exposed Cu2O facet, with a more pronounced improvement observed on the (111) surface. Guided by these findings, a cubic-like Ni/Cu2O structure was rationally designed and synthesized via a facile one-pot reduction method, serving as an efficient electrocatalyst for NO3RR. Owing to the sufficient supply of *H, Ni/Cu2O exhibits more efficient NO3− conversion to NH3 and significantly reduced NO2− accumulation compared to pristine Cu2O. Therefore, the optimized Ni/Cu2O delivers an impressive NH3 FE of 91.11 ± 3.43% and an NH3 yield rate of 7.81 ± 0.53 mg h−1 mgcat−1 at −0.9 V vs. RHE, along with excellent stability over 20 cycles. Furthermore, XPS analysis reveals that the amount of oxygen vacancy-related active sites is increased and further contributes to effective NO3− conversion. This study highlights a surface engineering strategy for developing efficient non-noble metal oxide catalysts, offering valuable insights for advancing sustainable electrocatalytic nitrate reduction systems.

      2 Results and discussion

      2.1 Theoretical calculation

      We hypothesize that Ni introduction into Cu2O substrate can modulate the surface electronic structure of the catalyst by altering the position of the d-band center, thereby optimizing the adsorption strength of intermediates and complementing the NO3− activation and deoxygenation capacities of an obtained catalyst. To test our idea, we first elucidate electronic structures of non-reconstructed Cu2O(111) and Ni-doped Cu2O(111). Cu2O(111) is chosen because the previous work reported that this facet shows the highest NO3RR catalytic activity.26  Subsequently, to validate the proposed design strategy, we evaluate the NO3RR activity and *H generation from water dissociation on both Cu2O(111) and Cu2O(001) surfaces with Ni incorporation. These two facets were chosen because they are the most representative low-index surfaces of Cu2O and possess markedly different atomic and electronic structures. Comparing their catalytic behavior enables us to examine both the facet dependence of NO3RR activity and the generality of the Ni-doping strategy for enhancing catalytic performance.

      DFT calculations are carried out using the Quantum ESPRESSO package to investigate NO3RR mechanism on the Cu2O(111) and Cu2O(001) surface.27  The revised Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (RPBE) exchange-correlation functional combined with Grimme's third-generation van der Waals dispersion correction (D3) is employed to elucidate the exchange correlation energy and long-range dispersion interactions.28,29  Ultrasoft pseudopotentials are used to describe the core-valence electron interactions, with kinetic energy cutoffs of 40 Ry for the plane-wave basis set and 320 Ry for the augmented charge density.30  The Cu2O(111) surface was modeled using an unreconstructed four-layer p(2 × 2) slab, with the bottom two layers fixed and the upper two layers relaxed. The Cu2O(001) surface was represented by a Cu-terminated three-layer p(2 × 2) slab with bottommost layers fixed. A vacuum region of 15 Å was introduced perpendicular to the surface to eliminate interactions between periodic images. The Ni doped Cu2O catalyst was modeled by considering Cu2O(111) and (001) slabs in which a single surface Cu atom was substituted by a Ni atom. Brillouin zone integrations are performed using a Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh of 2 × 2 × 1.31  Electronic occupations are treated using the Marzari-Vanderbilt cold smearing scheme with a smearing width of 0.01 Ry. All surface structures and reaction intermediates involved in the NO3RR pathway are fully optimized until the residual forces on each atom was below the 10−3 Ry/Bohr−1. For simplicity, we use total energy to construct the NO3RR pathway.32 

      As shown in Fig. 1a, on the topmost Cu2O(111) surface, two distinct types of Cu species are present: coordinatively saturated Cu (Cucsa), which retains twofold coordination with neighboring O atoms, and coordinatively unsaturated Cu (Cucus), which exhibits only single coordination with the underlying O atom. Owing to the unsaturated bonding environment of Cucus, its d-band center is significantly upshifted from −2.40 eV for Cucsa to −1.85 eV for Cucus, indicating that Cucus provides more chemically active sites for the NO3RR. The most stable substitution site for Ni on Cu2O(111) is at the Cucus position (Fig. 1b). As shown by the projected density of states (PDOS) in Fig. 1b, the Ni d-band is further shifted toward the Fermi level (EF), accompanied by an increased density of states near EF. This electronic modulation strengthens the interaction between the catalyst surface and N-intermediates, thereby promoting charge transfer and accelerating the subsequent hydrogenation steps.

      The calculated Gibbs free energy diagrams for NO3RR and *H generation from H2O on Cu2O(111), Ni/Cu2O(111), Cu2O(001), and Ni/Cu2O(001) are shown in Fig. 1c and d. First, NO3 preferentially adsorbs on the introduced Ni sites of Ni-doped Cu2O surfaces, providing highly active centers for NO3RR. In addition, Ni-induced electronic structure modulation facilitates the *NO2 → *NO conversion on both Cu2O(111) and Cu2O(001) by lowering the corresponding reaction free energy, although the effect is more pronounced on the Cu2O(111) surface. Specifically, the free energy of the *NO2 → *NO step decreases from −1.10 to −2.08 eV upon Ni incorporation, reflecting facilitated NOx deoxygenation arising from stronger *NO adsorption at the Ni site. While all elementary steps on pristine Cu2O(111) are thermodynamically downhill, Ni/Cu2O(111) exhibits a modest uphill free-energy change of 0.32 eV for the *NO → *NOH hydrogenation step, which becomes the potential-determining step. As discussed below, Ni substitution lowers the free energy of H2O dissociation, increasing surface *H availability, which is expected to facilitate the *NO → *NOH hydrogenation step and help overcome the small potential-determining barrier.33,34  A similar but weaker effect is observed on Cu2O(001), where Ni substitution lowers the free energy of the *NO2 → *NO step from −1.08 to −1.38 eV while slightly increasing that of the final *NH2 → *NH3 hydrogenation step from 0.60 to 0.67 eV. Furthermore, Ni substitution facilitates *H generation, particularly on Cu2O(111), where the H2O dissociation free energy decreases from 0.84 to 0.20 eV, compared with a smaller change from −2.26 to −2.40 eV on Cu2O(001) (Fig. 1d). The resulting increase in surface *H availability, together with enhanced NOx deoxygenation, highlights the synergistic role of Ni substitution in promoting NO3RR toward NH3 formation, with the strongest enhancement observed on the Cu2O(111) facet.

      Although the enhancement is more pronounced on the Cu2O(111) facet, Ni substitution consistently improves the catalytic performance of both Cu2O(111) and Cu2O(001). These results indicate that the beneficial effect of Ni incorporation arises primarily from Ni-induced electronic structure modulation and the creation of active sites for NO3RR, as NO3 adsorption is thermodynamically favored on the Ni sites, rather than from a specific crystal orientation. Consequently, Ni incorporation universally enhances the intrinsic catalytic activity of Cu2O surfaces toward NO3RR.

      In addition, the enhanced NO3RR performance of Ni/Cu2O originates from the modulation of the local electronic structure induced by Ni incorporation that enhances NO adsorption. As revealed by the PDOS analysis, Ni substitution shifts the d-band center closer to the Fermi level compared with pristine Cu2O (Fig. 1a and b), leading to stronger hybridization between the metal 3d states and the NO 2π* antibonding orbitals (Fig. 1e and f). This effect stabilizes adsorbed NO, as evidenced by the larger electron transfer to *NO on Ni/Cu2O(111) (−0.29e) relative to Cu2O(111) (−0.17e). This enhanced orbital overlap strengthens *NO adsorption, stabilizes key reaction intermediates, and provides a clear electronic origin for the improved *NO2 → *NO conversion and subsequent hydrogenation observed on Ni/Cu2O(111).

      2.2 Characterization

      Theoretical calculations indicate that water molecules can be readily dissociated on the Ni/Cu2O surface to generate *H species, which are then efficiently transferred to *NO intermediates with sufficient residence time, promoting the formation of *NOH (Fig. 2a). This process prevents the accumulation of nitrogen-containing intermediates and helps decouple the scaling relationship between the HER and NO3RR, enabling Ni/Cu2O to outperform pristine Cu2O.

      In this work, cubic-like Cu2O particles are successfully prepared through a one-pot chemical reduction route based on the method reported by Phu et al., with slight modifications.16  Specifically, the introduction of KOH into the copper tartrate complex results in the formation of [Cu(C4H4O6)2]2−, which is subsequently reduced by glucose under hydrothermal conditions to produce cubic-shaped Cu2O. Furthermore, Ni/Cu2O samples are synthesized under similar conditions by introducing a 2 mmol Ni precursor after the formation of Cu2O, aiming to slightly modify the surface while largely preserving the original cubic morphology, as illustrated in Fig. 2b. The detailed experiments and characterization are presented fully in SI. The single-component Ni catalyst cannot be synthesized from Ni precursor, thus, in this work, there are not available characterizations of single-component Ni catalyst. Given that cubic Cu2O predominantly exposes the (001) facet and that this surface exhibits the lower NO3RR activity than Cu2O(111), Cu2O(001) was chosen as a model system to test whether Ni doping can enhance NO3RR.

      The morphology of pristine Cu2O with a cubic-like structure is illustrated by Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FE-SEM) (Fig. 2c). After Ni introduction, the Ni/Cu2O sample exhibits a noticeably rougher surface, which can be attributed to the interaction and collision of Ni ions with the Cu2O surface during catalyst formation process (Fig. 2e). This increased surface roughness is expected to create more exposed active sites, thereby enhancing the specific surface area and improving NO3RR performance. High resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) images of Cu2O and Ni/Cu2O are presented in Fig. 2d and h, respectively. In Fig. 2d, apparent lattice fringes with interplanar spacings of 0.31 nm and 0.25 nm are assigned to the (110) and (111) planes of cubic Cu2O.16  For the Ni/Cu2O sample, lattice spacings of 0.25 nm and 0.22 nm correspond to the Cu2O (111) and (200) planes, respectively, confirming that the primary Cu2O phase is preserved after Ni introduction (Fig. 2f).35  Additionally, elemental mapping is employed to verify the composition and spatial distribution of elements. As shown in Fig. 2g, Cu and O are uniformly distributed in pristine Cu2O. In the case of Ni/Cu2O (Fig. 2h), Cu, O, and Ni elements are homogeneously dispersed throughout the entire structure, indicating successful incorporation of Ni without phase segregation.

      The XRD pattern of the synthesized sample is consistent with that of pure Cu2O, showing a dominant (111) orientation with a characteristic peak at 2θ = 36.45° (JCPDS no. 05-0667).36  Additional diffraction peaks observed at 29.62°, 42.36°, 61.42°, 73.55°, and 77.44° can be indexed to the (110), (200), (220), (311), and (222) planes of distorted face-centered cubic (fcc) Cu2O, respectively (Fig. 3a).37  Notably, no diffraction signals corresponding to crystalline Ni phases are detected, which can be attributed to the very low concentration of the Ni precursor used during synthesis. Additionally, as shown in Fig. 3b, Ni/Cu2O catalyst exhibits a surface area of 14.291 m2 g−1, which is significantly greater than that of pristine Cu2O (4.449 m2 g−1), demonstrating that Ni doping effectively enhances the surface area of the Cu2O substrate. The detailed nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherm parameters of all prepared samples are summarized in Table S1, SI.

      X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is employed to investigate the surface composition and chemical states of the synthesized Cu2O and Ni/Cu2O catalysts. C 1s XPS spectra are first used for calibration, referencing the C–C peak at 284.8 eV (Fig. S1, SI). As presented in Fig. 3c, Ni/Cu2O shows that the Ni 2p XPS spectrum displays four fitted peaks located at approximately 856, 873, 860, and 878 eV, corresponding to Ni 2p3/2, Ni 2p1/2, and their associated satellite peaks, respectively.38–40  In contrast, no Ni 2p signal is detected for pristine Cu2O, confirming the absence of Ni in that sample. In addition, the Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 XPS spectra (Fig. 3d) can be deconvoluted into two primary components at ∼932.4 eV, ∼933.9 eV, ∼952.3 eV, and ∼953.4 eV which are assigned to Cu0/1+ 2p3/2, Cu2+ 2p3/2, Cu0/1+ 2p1/2, and Cu2+ 2p1/2 species, respectively.41  Compared with bare Cu2O, a slight shift in the Cu0/1+ peak is observed for Ni/Cu2O, suggesting that Ni incorporation alters the electronic environment of Cu and induces charge redistribution on the surface (Fig. S2, SI).42,43  Based on these spectra, the Cu0/1+/Cu2+ ratios are calculated to be 1.270 for Cu2O and 1.324 for Ni/Cu2O. Previous studies have shown that Cu+/Cu0 pairs facilitate rapid kinetics for the conversion of *NO3− to *NO2−.44,45  Moreover, these species enhance *H generation and accumulation, thereby promoting subsequent hydrogenation steps and improving both activity and selectivity toward NH3 formation in NO3RR. To further clarify the oxidation states of Cu, Auger electron spectroscopy was performed by analyzing the Cu LMM region (Fig. 3e). Deconvoluted peaks at 918.6, 916.8, and 914.2 eV correspond to Cu0, Cu+, and Cu(1+δ)+ species, respectively.46,47  The coexistence of multiple oxidation states of Cu is beneficial for NO3RR, as it provides abundant interfacial active sites and enhances the overall catalytic performance.48,49  As depicted in Fig. 3f, O 1s XPS spectra can be deconvoluted into three deconvoluted peaks at 530.2, 531.6, and 533.3 eV, which are assigned to lattice oxygen (OL), surface hydroxyl/chemisorbed water (OH−sur/H2Ochem), and adsorbed oxygen species (Oads), respectively.50,51  Notably, XPS analysis reveals that the proportion of OH−sur/H2Ochem in Ni/Cu2O is ∼1.4 times higher than that in pristine Cu2O, indicating a greater abundance of oxygen-deficient active sites on the Ni/Cu2O surface. The increased surface hydroxyl and chemisorbed water coverage are consistent with a higher density of oxygen-vacancy (OV)-related active sites on the Ni/Cu2O surface.51  Enhancing the amount of OH−sur/H2Ochem active sites by Ni doping provides an effective strategy to enhance NO3RR performance.52–54  These defect sites can serve as active centers that facilitate electron transfer, strengthen NO3− adsorption, weaken N═O bonds, activate nitrogen-containing intermediates, and suppress the formation of undesired byproducts.55,56  Consequently, Ni/Cu2O catalysts with abundant OV-related active sites are expected to significantly improve its electrocatalytic activity toward nitrate reduction. Detailed percentages of the fitted peaks for Cu 2p XPS, Cu LMM, and O 1s XPS spectra are summarized in Table S2 (SI). As a result, the combined XRD, HR-TEM, XPS, and EDS results strongly suggest that Ni is highly dispersed within or on the Cu2O structure rather than forming a separate detectable crystalline phase.

      2.3 Nitrate-to-ammonia electrochemical reduction

      Before each NO3RR measurement, the electrochemical system is pre-activated by performing three cyclic voltammetry (CV) cycles at a scan rate of 100 mV s−1. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) for NO3RR is first carried out in 0.1 M K2SO4 electrolyte, with and without adding 0.1 M KNO3, over a potential range from 0.2 to −0.9 V vs. RHE. As presented in Fig. 4a, Ni/Cu2O catalyst shows a pronounced increase in current density after introducing NO3−, indicating that NO3RR proceeds more efficiently than the competing HER. During the cathodic scan, two distinct reduction peaks are observed at 0 to −0.3 and −0.5 to −0.7 V vs. RHE, labeled as R1 and R2, respectively. The R1 peak appears only in NO3−-containing electrolyte and disappears when NO3− is replaced by an equivalent concentration of NO2−, suggesting that it corresponds to the reduction of NO3− to NO2−. In contrast, the R2 feature at more negative potentials is attributed to the further reduction of NO2− to NH3 (NO2RR), as supported by the similar voltammetric behavior observed in both NO3−– and NO2−-containing electrolytes.57  Although pristine Cu2O exhibits a comparable trend, its current density is notably lower than that of Ni/Cu2O (Fig. S3, SI). Additionally, the negligible current response from bare carbon cloth confirms that the catalytic activity mainly originates from the intrinsic properties of active materials rather than the substrate (Fig. S4, SI).

      To optimize the catalyst composition, a series of Ni/Cu2O catalysts with different Ni loadings (Ni/Cu2O_1 mmol Ni, Ni/Cu2O_2 mmol Ni, and Ni/Cu2O_3 mmol Ni) were systematically evaluated. Subsequently, the double-layer capacitance (Cdl) was estimated from CV measurements at different scan rates within the non-faradaic region (Fig. S5, SI). The results reveal that Ni/Cu2O_2 mmol Ni exhibits the largest Cdl value (2.12 mF cm−2), compared with Ni/Cu2O_1 mmol Ni (1.31 mF cm−2), Ni/Cu2O_3 mmol Ni (1.20 mV dec−1 mF cm−2), and Cu2O (0.604 mF cm−2), indicating more favorable reaction kinetics and a higher density of accessible active sites during NO3RR (Fig. 4b and S6, SI).

      To assess the NO3RR activity, potentiostatic electrolysis was carried out over a potential range from −0.4 to −1.1 V vs. RHE (Fig. S7, SI). The concentrations of produced NH3, NO2−, and residual NO3− in the electrolyte were quantified using UV-vis spectroscopy (Fig. S8, SI). As shown in Fig. 4c and d, the NH3 yield rate increases nearly linearly with more negative applied potentials, whereas the NH3 FE exhibits a volcano-type trend due to the competing HER.58,59  Notably, Ni/Cu2O achieves NH3 FE of 91.11 ± 3.43% and NH3 yield rate of 7.81 ± 0.53 mg h−1 mgcat−1 at −0.9 V vs. RHE, outperforming pristine Cu2O (NH3 FE of 65.79 ± 2.71% and NH3 yield rate of 6.02 ± 0.492 mg h−1 mgcat−1). Among them, Ni/Cu2O_1 mmol Ni delivers a NH3 FE of 79.11 ± 4.60% and a yield rate of 6.77 ± 0.64 mg h−1 mgcat−1, while Ni/Cu2O_3 mmol Ni achieves a NH3 FE of 85.52 ± 5.64% and an NH3 yield rate of 9.41 ± 0.82 mg h−1 mgcat−1. As-prepared Ni/Cu2O catalysts exhibit superior NO3RR performance compared to bare Cu2O, confirming the beneficial role of Ni incorporation (Fig. S9, SI). Moreover, as-prepared Ni/Cu2O catalysts show lower NO2− yield and FE than Cu2O, indicating more efficient conversion of NO2− intermediates (Fig. S10, SI). Based on these findings, Ni/Cu2O_2 mmol Ni is identified as the optimal catalyst and is hereafter referred to as Ni/Cu2O. This sample was selected for subsequent structural characterization and detailed electrochemical investigations.

      To gain deeper insight into the NO3RR mechanism on Cu2O and Ni/Cu2O, operando EIS is employed. During NO3RR, the HER acts as an unavoidable pathway, proceeding via three elementary steps: the Volmer step (H2O + e− → H* + OH−), the Heyrovsky step (H2O + H* + e− → H2 + OH−), and the Tafel step (2H* → H2).60  In Bode plots, the low- and mid-frequency regions are generally assigned to the Volmer and Heyrovsky steps, respectively, while the Tafel step is not detectable due to the absence of electron transfer.61  Moreover, the magnitude of the phase angle peak reflects the rate of interfacial electron transfer. As illustrated in Fig. 5a, in NO3−-free electrolyte, the Bode plot shows characteristic peaks in the low-frequency range (0.1–1.0 Hz). With increasing negatively applied potential, these peaks gradually shift toward higher frequencies, accompanied by a slight decrease in phase angle, which can be attributed to enhanced *H generation during HER. A similar trend is observed for Ni/Cu2O, but with a slightly reduced phase angle, indicating more efficient *H formation in Ni/Cu2O (Fig. 5b). Conversely, when NO3− is present, the HER-related features diminish within the same potential window, and the characteristic peaks move to a higher frequency region (1.0–100 Hz), suggesting that NO3RR becomes the dominant process on the Cu2O surface (Fig. 5c). Notably, for Ni/Cu2O, the peak positions shift further toward higher frequencies and exhibit lower phase angles compared to bare Cu2O under identical conditions. This behavior indicates faster interfacial charge transfer, more efficient utilization of H* by NO3−, and accelerated NO3RR kinetics (Fig. 5d). Additionally, Nyquist plots reveal that Ni/Cu2O presents smaller semicircle diameters than Cu2O in both the absence and presence of NO3− at same working potentials (Fig. S11, SI), confirming that Ni/Cu2O effectively reduces the overall charge-transfer resistance and enhances electron transport during the NO3RR process than pristine Cu2O.62

      To examine the influence of NO3− concentration on NO3RR performance, a series of electrochemical measurements are carried out using Ni/Cu2O in 0.1 M K2SO4 electrolyte with NO3− concentrations ranging from 0.05 to 0.5 M (Fig. 6a and S12a, SI). The NH3 yield rate increases progressively with rising NO3− concentration, which can be attributed to the greater availability of NO3− species in the electrolyte. However, NH3 FE declining trend at higher concentrations, decreasing to approximately 81.8%, 80.8%, and 78.9% at 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5 M NO3−, respectively, after remaining above ∼90% at 0.05, 0.1, and 0.2 M. This decrease is mainly due to the insufficient supply of active *H species under high nitrate concentrations. In addition, increasing NO3− concentration can elevate mass transfer resistance, hindering the diffusion of NO3− ions to the catalyst surface. This limitation adversely affects both adsorption at active sites and subsequent reduction steps, leading to incomplete conversion and the formation of NO2− byproducts (Fig. S13a, SI). The concentration of the supporting electrolyte also plays a crucial role in NO3RR performance. With an optimized K2SO4 concentration (≤0.1 M), convenient electron and ion transport could contribute to improved catalytic activity. As shown in Fig. 6b and S13b (SI), further increasing SO42− concentration beyond 0.1 M results in a noticeable decline in NH3 FE due to excessive supporting electrolyte suppresses the interaction between NO3− and catalytic active sites and impedes product desorption, thereby introducing kinetic limitations. Moreover, to investigate the effect of pH, the NO3RR performance of Ni/Cu2O is evaluated in neutral (0.1 M K2SO4 + 0.1 M KNO3, pH = 6.7) and alkaline (0.1 M KOH + 0.1 M KNO3, pH = 12.5) electrolytes at −0.9 V vs. RHE (Fig. 6c). The catalyst exhibits superior performance in alkaline conditions, achieving a maximum NH3 yield rate of 9.70 ± 0.91 mg h−1 mgcat−1 and a NH3 FE of 96.33 ± 4.15%, significantly higher than in neutral media. This improvement suggests that alkaline environments facilitate charge transfer and suppress the competing HER on Ni/Cu2O. However, the catalyst demonstrates limited stability under alkaline conditions, as evidenced by the rapid decay in current density observed in i-t curves (Fig. S14, SI).

      The long-term stability of Ni/Cu2O was assessed by chronoamperometric measurements at −0.9 V vs. RHE over 20 successive cycles (Fig. 6d). The catalyst maintains a nearly constant current density throughout the test after refreshing NO3− electrolyte, demonstrating excellent durability. Although slight variations in NH3 FE and yield rate are observed, the performance remains above 84% for NH3 FE and greater than 7 mg h−1 mgcat−1 after 20 cycles. These minor fluctuations are likely caused by minor experimental inconsistencies between cycles, such as changes in electrode distance or local electrolyte conditions, which can influence current density and thus affect NH3 FE and yield. For comparison, pristine Cu2O shows lower stability, retaining NH3 yield rates of ∼4.95 mg h−1 mgcat−1 and FE above 58% after 10 cycles (Fig. S15, SI). As a result, Ni/Cu2O exhibits superior reproducibility and durability for prolonged NO3RR operation.       To confirm that the detected NH3 originates exclusively from nitrate reduction, control experiments were conducted in both blank and NO3−-containing electrolytes using either Ni/Cu2O or carbon cloth as the working electrode. As shown in Fig. S16 (SI), no detectable NH3 is observed in 0.1 M K2SO4 when Ni/Cu2O is used, indicating that NH3 formation is directly derived from NO3−. In addition, the system employing carbon cloth as the working electrode produces only a negligible amount of NH3 (0.253 ppm) compared to the significantly higher concentration (7.807 ppm) obtained with Ni/Cu2O. This result confirms that the enhanced NO3RR activity primarily arises from the intrinsic catalytic properties of the Ni/Cu2O material.

      After the NO3RR, the Ni/Cu2O catalyst exhibited moderate structural stability, largely retaining its cubic morphology with Ni conformally distributed on the Cu2O surface (Fig. S17, SI). The XPS spectra of Ni/Cu2O after the NO3RR showed only minor changes compared with those before the reaction, indicating that the catalyst largely preserved its phase structure during the electrochemical process (Fig. S18, SI). Compared with the XPS Cu 2p spectrum before the NO3RR, the decreased percentage of Cu2+ in the Cu 2p spectrum and the lower binding energy of the Ni 2p spectrum after the NO3RR suggest that Cu2+ and Ni2+ were partially reduced to Cu+/Cu0 and Ni(2−δ)+, respectively, during the reaction. Furthermore, after the NO3RR electrolysis, the amount of OHsur−/H2Ochem, which is correlated with the density of oxygen vacancy-related active sites, decreased, indicating that these hydroxyl- and water-related surface species also serve as active sites for the NO3RR.81 

      2.4 Discusion on limitation of this work

      While the present study provides strong evidence that Ni incorporation enhances the NO3RR performance of Cu2O through electronic-structure modulation and defect engineering, several limitations should be acknowledged:

      First, our theoretical results consistently indicate that the beneficial effect of Ni incorporation is not limited to a specific crystal orientation but universally enhances the intrinsic NO3RR activity of Cu2O through electronic structure modulation, although the magnitude of the enhancement varies between different surface terminations. In this work, our DFT calculations demonstrate this enhancement on both Cu2O(111) and Cu2O(001). Experimentally, however, only cubic-like Cu2O catalysts, which predominantly expose the Cu2O(001) facet, were synthesized and evaluated. Therefore, experimental validation of the predicted enhancement on other Cu2O facets remains beyond the scope of the present study. Future investigations employing facet-controlled Cu2O catalysts would provide valuable verification of the generality of the proposed Ni-induced electronic modulation mechanism.

      Second, the qualitative conclusion on increase in OV-related active sites are solely based on XPS spectra. Future studies employing electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy could provide direct experimental evidence for active hydrogen generation and oxygen-vacancy-related defect sites, further validating the proposed mechanism.

      Third, the NO3RR reaction mechanism was elucidated solely by DFT calculations and ex situ techniques. Direct identification of surface intermediates under operating conditions remains challenging. Advanced operando techniques, such as in situ Raman spectroscopy, infrared spectroscopy, or X-ray absorption spectroscopy, would provide valuable insights into the evolution of reaction intermediates and the dynamic structural changes of active sites during NO3RR. Combining those in situ techniques with advanced DFT calculations that taken into account the effects of dynamic surface reconstruction, catalytic environment would enable a more realistic description of NO3RR activity.57,82–84 

      3 Conclusions

      In this work, a series of Ni-doped Cu2O catalysts with well-defined cubic architecture were successfully synthesized via a facile one-pot reduction approach. DFT calculations, combined with comprehensive experimental characterization, reveal that Ni doped Cu2O induces electronic structure modulation, enhances water dissociation to generate surface *H species, and optimizes the adsorption and hydrogenation of nitrogen-containing intermediates, namely *NO, thereby facilitating the stepwise reduction of NO3− to NH3, while mitigating intermediate accumulation and suppressing the parasitic HER. In addition, as revealed by XPS and HR-TEM, Ni doping increases the concentration of OV-related active sites and enhances surface roughness, resulting in a larger density of accessible catalytic sites. Ni/Cu2O with substantially enhanced NO3RR activity and NH3 selectivity while suppressing the competing HER. Electrochemical evaluations demonstrate that the optimized Ni/Cu2O catalyst achieves a high NH3 FE of 91.11 ± 3.43% and an NH3 yield rate of 7.81 ± 0.53 mg h−1 mgcat−1 at −0.9 V vs. RHE, significantly outperforming pristine Cu2O. Operando EIS further demonstrates that Ni/Cu2O could reduce interfacial charge-transfer resistance and accelerate reaction kinetics. This study provides fundamental insights into the structure–activity relationship of enriched OV-Ni/Cu2O catalyst and highlights an effective strategy for the rational design of efficient, earth-abundant electrocatalysts for sustainable nitrate-to-ammonia conversion.
