      Direct ethanol fuel cells (DEFCs) have emerged as promising sustainable energy alternatives, with noble metals such as Pd and Pt traditionally serving as key catalysts for the ethanol oxidation reaction (EOR). In this study, a cost-effective Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid was synthesized and evaluated as a non-noble metal electrocatalyst for the EOR. Morphological and structural analyses revealed an average size of ∼118.9 nm and a lattice fringe spacing of 0.253 nm, as observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) confirmed the mixed valence state of Cu, and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analysis provided quantitative insights into C, O, and Cu distributions. Electrochemical evaluations, including cyclic voltammetry (CV) and chronoamperometry (i–t), revealed a low onset potential of 0.45 V vs. SCE and a peak current density of 37.45 mA cm−2 at 0.72 V vs. SCE in solution of 1.0 M ethanol in 1.0 M KOH for ethanol oxidation. Additionally, the proposed electrocatalytic system exhibited excellent stability, maintaining its performance over 10 000 s of operation. These results highlight the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid as a promising electrocatalyst for efficient ethanol oxidation in DEFCs.

      Introduction

      The global energy crisis, coupled with increasing concern over environmental degradation and the continued reliance on non-renewable fossil fuels, underscores the urgent need for the development of clean and efficient energy conversion technologies.1,2  Among the numerous energy sources, fuel cells due to their peculiar properties are on the verge of creating vast revolutionary changes and are solemnly considered as sustainable energy conversion devices.3,4  DEFCs in particular have garnered significant attention owing to ethanol's favourable properties, including low toxicity, ease of energy storage and transport, high volumetric energy density, and most importantly its renewable origin from biomass feedstocks such as agricultural, forestry, and urban residues.5–8  Ethanol based systems also offer an advantage over hydrogen fuel cells by mitigating the challenges associated with hydrogen storage, its transportation and infrastructure.9  Despite this, low efficiency of ethanol oxidation, largely due to the difficulty in breaking the C–C bond, has indeed posed a significant challenge to the commercialization of DEFCs. This challenge affects the overall energy output and efficiency of the cells, making it a critical area for further research and development.10–12 

      Precious metals such as platinum (Pt) and palladium (Pd) demonstrated benchmark catalytic performance in ethanol oxidation, in DEFCs, due to their good specific activity.13,14  However, their scarcity, and vulnerability to CO poisoning species, severely restricts their effectiveness.15,16  To address these challenges, extensive research has explored bimetallic and trimetallic alloy catalysts, which not only reduce the noble metal content but also enhance the electrocatalytic performance and CO tolerance.17–19  In recent years, transition metal based systems particularly Cu, Co, and Ni, have been widely investigated in combination with noble metals due to their cost effectiveness, tunable electronic properties and earth abundance. Among them, Ni-based materials have attracted considerable attention due to the reversible Ni2+/Ni3+ redox couple, which facilitates the adsorption and oxidation of ethanol derived intermediates in alkaline medium. For example, Mali et al. synthesized Ni/NiO–rGO hybrid nanostructures using a sol–gel technique, which showed improved electrochemical sensitivity for ethanol detection at ambient temperature. The proposed sensor is appropriate for real-sample ethanol detection applications due to its high selectivity, low oxidation potential of 0.33 V vs. SCE, and good diffusion-controlled response.20  Similar results have been reported for other Ni-based electrocatalysts, where the combination of abundant active sites and improved electron transport promotes ethanol oxidation kinetics.21–23 

      Co-based catalysts have also been widely investigated because of their multiple oxidation states, high electrochemical activity and ability to modify surface electronic properties. Co-containing oxides, hydroxides and bimetallic composites have shown improved catalytic performance by facilitating charge transfer. Furthermore, incorporation of Co into multi-metallic systems often enhances catalyst stability and resistance towards poisoning species, making Co an attractive component in EOR electrocatalysts.24–26  For instance, Tsui et al. enhanced the performance of Pd–Co by utilizing an alloy of Pd and Co. The oxyphilic Co supplies ample OH− to the EOR, accelerating the rate limiting step at low overpotential under alkaline conditions.

      Among all the transition metals, Cu and Cu based hybrids have been widely used as anode materials in the ethanol oxidation reaction due to the balance between catalyst activity and CO poison resistance.27,28  Cu has also been considered for its low cost and earth abundance. For instance, Santacesaria et al. found that copper/copper chromite (Cu/Cu2Cr2Ox) catalysts based on alumina and aided by appropriate additives had great selectivity and satisfactory conversion for ethanol dehydrogenation to ethyl acetate. The promoters serve to avoid metal sintering and establish a favourable acid–base environment, which increases both activity and stability.29  Witzke et al. used inhibition studies, steady-state kinetics, and in situ X-ray absorption spectroscopy to clarify the functions of Cu species in the catalytic process. According to their findings, Cu0 mostly drives the dehydrogenation stages, whereas Cuδ+ sites actively participate in the synthesis of C4H8O2 from C2H4O and C2H5OH. This mechanistic route is further supported by the high association between the selectivity parameter γ and the Cu oxidation state ratio as well as the inhibition of ester formation by pyridine.30  Besides, Wu et al. investigated the elementary reaction of EOR on Cu(100) using the GGA/PBE method, demonstrating the ability of Cu to activate C–C bond cleavage in CH2CO intermediates.31  H. Niu et al. developed a MOF-derived hierarchical porous Cu/Cu2O/C catalyst with uniformly dispersed nanoparticles, exhibiting high activity for NaBH4-assisted 4-nitrophenol reduction.32 

      More importantly, Cu based bimetallic catalysts exhibit greater performance towards the EOR.33,34  Haan et al. modified the surface of Pd by introducing Cu for oxidation of formate and ethanol in alkaline fuel cells, and this study showed that the addition of Cu induces the electronic effect on the Pd surface, leading to improved oxidation processes of ethanol in alkaline medium.35  Moreover, Miao et al. designed a highly efficient and stable Cu–Ni alloy, which showed an excellent anodic peak current density of 10 mA cm−2 in 0.2 M ethanol and retained 95% of its initial performance after 2000 s, indicating long-term stability.36  In addition to this, a detailed comparative study of Pt–Cu bimetallic nano-dendrites over Pt black for the methanol oxidation reaction (MOR) was also reported.37  Enhanced performance of bimetallic electrocatalysts upon alloying with Cu is generally attributed to the oxyphilic nature of the Cu surface, which promotes the oxidation of adsorbed intermediates or poisoning species through a bifunctional mechanism, thereby improving CO tolerance of the catalyst.38,39  Additionally, Cu has been reported to enhance the reaction rate for the electrochemical oxidation of ethanol when combined with various conductive support catalysts.40,41  Thus, due to better catalytic activity and low cost, Cu is found to be a potential candidate for the EOR. In line with this, herein, Cu metal in combination with a carbon material for the EOR has been studied. Accordingly, the potential use of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid as an efficient electrocatalyst for EOR studies was explored. The catalyst exhibits outstanding performance for the EOR with a peak current density of 37.45 mA cm−2 at a peak potential of 0.72 V vs. SCE. The results obtained during the oxidation of ethanol show that the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid provides excellent electrocatalytic activity and stability. Building on these advances, this study explores the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid as a candidate electrocatalyst, with an emphasis on its structural simplicity, scalability, and potential for future modifications. This report provides a concise summary of future research directions related to ethanol oxidation on Cu–C nanohybrids.

      Results and discussion

      Fig. 1(a) and (b) show the field emission scanning electron micrograph (FE-SEM) exhibiting the surface topography of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid. These nanoparticles are closely connected with each other, and there is a formation of clusters and agglomeration with each other, and this could be due to the poly-functional nature of ascorbic acid molecules. The elemental analysis of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid is carried out by TEM-EDS mapping and accordingly, Fig. 1(c)–(e) show the corresponding elemental maps for Cu, C, and O, demonstrating the homogeneous distribution. Energy dispersive analysis of X-ray (EDAX) is a microanalytical technique for the quantitative analysis of elements. Fig. 1(f) shows the EDAX pattern of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid and its elemental composition and atomic percentage are found to be Cu (7.98%), C (34.46%), and O (57.65%) supporting earlier findings on the formation of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid. The Cu content (7.98%) was within the expected composition range for the synthesis approach and intentionally targeted to balance catalytic activity and stability. This composition is consistent with prior reports on Cu-containing bimetallic systems, e.g. the CO2RR or HER, where the moderate Cu content has been shown to enhance performance by modulating the electronic structure and binding strength of intermediates.42,43

      Information on the morphologies and structures of the synthesized nanoparticle can be gleaned from Fig. 2. The TEM image of Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid is shown in Fig. 2(a), revealing its internal structure. It consists of a number of spherical nanoparticles which are nanometres in size and of nearly equal dimensions. The synthesized nanohybrids have an average diameter of ∼118.9 nm, as shown in the particle size distribution curve enclosed in Fig. 2(a), which places them near the upper limit of the nanoscale regime. Despite an overall particle size greater than 100 nm, the material exhibits distinct nanoscale characteristics, arising from the coexistence of nanosized crystallites and highly nanostructured Cu/Cu2O interfaces. Furthermore, the zoomed-in HR-TEM image of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid in Fig. 2(b) clearly depicts the formation of a coating or a layer of carbon surrounding Cu, which indicates the successful formation of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid. Furthermore, the magnified portion of Fig. 2(b) shown in Fig. 2(c) also supports the coating of carbon on Cu. The HR-TEM pattern of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid shows well-resolved lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.253 nm as shown in Fig. 2(d). The multiple electron diffraction (ED) rings are observed in the SAED pattern of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid as shown in Fig. 2(e), indicating the polycrystallinity. The selected area diffraction pattern (SAED) shows distinct ring spacing corresponding to the (111) and (200) planes of Cu2O, in agreement with the XRD peaks at ∼36.5° and ∼ 42.2°, respectively.44

      X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis is used to extend the structural characterization. Accordingly, Fig. 3(a) shows the XRD pattern of the as synthesized nanohybrids having strong diffraction, confirming high crystallinity of the catalyst. The diffraction peaks observed at 2θ values of 43.27, 50.49, and 74.14 correspond to the (hkl) values of (111), (200), and (220) crystal planes of metallic Cu, respectively. These three peaks were quite consistent with those of the standard JCPDS Card No. 85-1326 for the standard spectrum of the pure FCC (face centered cubic) metallic Cu.45  Besides the metallic Cu peaks, several other diffraction peaks were also observed. The diffraction peaks correspond to the (110), (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222) planes of cupric oxide (Cu2O), respectively, indicating the formation of cubic copper(i) oxide nanocrystals. The XRD peaks observed for FCC copper(i) oxide are in good agreement with those of the JCPDS Card No. 05-667. The diffractogram evidently indicates that a mixture of two phases consisting of metallic Cu and copper(i) oxide (Cu2O) in combination with Cu was synthesized. Another fine peak at 2θ value 26.2 corresponds to the (002) plane of carbon, indicating partial graphitization of the carbon matrix. This may arise from the carbonization of the organic precursor, ascorbic acid, under thermal conditions. Overall, Cu2+ ions were reduced to Cu0 and Cu1+. The mean size of the crystalline Cu nanoparticles calculated from the major diffraction peaks using the Scherrer equation is about 14.4 nm. The higher intensity of the (111) Cu2O diffraction peak in comparison to the (111) FCC Cu peak suggests that a larger fraction of the nanoparticles is present in the Cu2O phase.

      Significantly, the FTIR spectra were recorded for Cu/Cu2O–C and shown in Fig. 3(b), within the region of 4000 cm−1 to 400 cm−1. In the FTIR spectra, peaks appearing at 3408 cm−1 correspond to O–H stretching vibrations, while the peaks at 1668 cm−1 and 1587 cm−1 correspond to C═O stretching vibrations. The –COOH bands at 1374 cm−1 and 1444 cm−1 arising from oxidized ascorbic acid are corroborated by the C 1s XPS peak at ∼288.2 eV (O–C═O), confirming the presence of surface carboxyl functionalities.46  These surface –COOH groups serve as anchoring and adsorption sites for ethanol molecules, facilitating their effective binding to the surface and subsequent electrochemical oxidation. Moreover, the strong band at 622 cm−1 is the Cu–O band of cuprous oxide (Cu2O).

      XPS analysis was further carried out to reveal the electronic structure of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid. Fig. 4(a) exhibits the full XPS survey spectrum shows elemental composition as C 1s, O 1s, and Cu 2p respectively. Fig. 4(b) presents the high-resolution Cu 2p XPS spectrum of the Cu/Cu2O–C catalyst. The Cu 2p3/2 peak centered at ∼932.1–932.4 eV and the corresponding Cu 2p1/2 peak at ∼953–955 eV are characteristic peaks of Cu0 or Cu+ species, indicating the coexistence of metallic Cu and Cu2O. Owing to the well-known overlap of Cu0 and Cu+ binding energies in the Cu 2p region, these two oxidation states cannot be unambiguously distinguished based solely on the Cu 2p spectra. Only very weak shake-up satellite features are observed in the 940–945 eV region, indicating the presence of a negligible amount of Cu(ii), which is attributed to slight surface oxidation during sample handling and exposure to air, while Cu+/Cu0 species remain dominant in the sample. Overall, the XPS results confirm that the catalyst surface is dominated by mixed Cu0/Cu+ states with a negligible Cu2+ content.47–50  Moreover, the O 1s XPS spectrum in Fig. 4(c) can be deconvoluted into distinct components. The intense peak at ∼532.8 eV is attributed to oxygen-containing functional groups associated with the carbon. The component at ∼531.5 eV corresponds to metal–hydroxide (Cu–OH) species, indicating surface hydroxylation. The higher binding energy peaks at ∼533.87 eV and ∼537.26 eV are assigned to carbonyl (O═C) and ester/carboxyl (O═C–O) functionalities respectively, arising from partially carbonised starch-derived species.50  The XPS results, particularly the absence of Cu 2p shake-up satellite peaks and the dominance of Cu 2p3/2 signals at ∼932 eV, unequivocally confirm that the catalyst surface consists predominantly of mixed Cu0/Cu+ states with no significant Cu(ii) contribution, in full agreement with the deconvoluted O 1s and C 1s spectra. Fig. 4(d) displays the high-resolution C 1s spectrum, which has been deconvoluted into a number of distinctive components. The component at ∼285.04 eV is associated with C–C/C–H bonds, whereas the prominent peak at ∼284.02 eV is attributed to C═C (sp2-hybridized carbon). The C–OH and O–C–O functions are responsible for the peak at about 286.09 eV. Carbonyl (C═O) groups are linked to the higher binding energy component at ∼287.30 eV, while carboxyl/ester (O–C═O) functionalities are linked to the peak at ∼288.37 eV. Furthermore, the weak feature detected at ∼290.02 eV is attributed to the π–π shake-up satellite, which indicates the presence of conjugated carbon domains in the carbonaceous framework.51

      Thermogravimetric analysis was carried out under an inert N2 atmosphere, as shown in Fig. S1(a); hence, the observed weight losses cannot be attributed to oxidative processes or CO2 evolution. The first weight loss (∼13.38%) observed below 100 °C corresponds to the removal of physically adsorbed moisture and trapped solvent molecules, as evidenced by the initial sharp decline in the TGA trace. The second and major weight-loss steps are instead associated with the thermal decomposition of organic and carbonaceous species present in the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid. Specifically, the weight loss of ∼13.32% between 100 and 200 °C arises from the decomposition of weakly bound organic residues originating from the ascorbic acid capping agent. The major weight loss of ∼29.77% between 200 and 530 °C corresponds to the decomposition of carbonaceous species derived from starch, which coats and supports the Cu/Cu2O nanoparticles. Under a N2 atmosphere, this process involves carbon decomposition and volatilisation, not CO2 formation. The magnitude of the second and third weight-loss steps correlates well with the carbon content introduced during synthesis, confirming the presence of a carbon-coated Cu/Cu2O nanohybrid. Carbonaceous carbon and thermally stable Cu/Cu2O species are responsible for the remaining mass of around 51.29 weight percent at 800 °C. Accordingly, the TGA results reflect carbon decomposition behaviour rather than nanoparticle phase transformation.52  The UV-visible absorption spectrum of the as-synthesized catalyst as shown in Fig. S1(b) exhibits absorption peaks at 257 and 455 nm. These absorption peaks are attributed to the presence of the cuprous oxide (Cu2O) phase of Cu. A similar existence of the absorption peaks for cuprous oxide has already been reported.53 

      Furthermore, BET-surface area measurement has been carried out for the determination of pores, pore size distribution and the surface area of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrids. The N2-adsorption/desorption isotherm observed is type-V in the Langmuir model as shown in Fig. S2(a), having relative pressure P/P0 ranging from 0.0 to 1.0. The pore size distributions of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrids were examined by using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) and Dubinin–Astakhov (DA) models and the average pore volume is 0.0262 cc per gm, the surface area is 31.314 m2 g−1 and the pore radius is 16.78 Å, as shown in Fig. S2(b) and (c), respectively. The results demonstrated that the material has a good surface area and absorption ability. In addition, Fig. S3(a) shows the Raman spectra of the catalyst, consisting of two characteristic bands. The band at ∼1320 cm−1 is the D band, which is caused by the induction of a significant defect or disorder on the catalyst surface and the G band corresponding to the partial graphitization of the precursor used is observed at ∼1575 cm−1, which is also in good agreement with the XRD data shown in Fig. 4(a).

      Electrochemical studies

      Electrochemical behaviour towards ethanol oxidation of the as synthesized Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid has been studied in detail. Accordingly, the main objective of the study is to investigate the oxidation of ethanol on the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid in an alkaline solution using cyclic voltammetry (CV) and chronoamperometry (i–t) studies.

      Accordingly, Fig. 5(a) depicts the cyclic voltammetric response for bare (i) GCE, (ii) the GCE modified Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid in 1.0 M KOH, and (iii) in 1.0 M ethanol in 1.0 M KOH solution at a scan rate of 50 mV s−1. The ethanol oxidation current density was obtained by dividing the peak current values by the geometrical surface area of the modified GC electrode. As expected, no response was obtained for bare GCE in 1.0 M KOH. However, the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid shows a signal at −0.26 V vs. SCE as shown in the inset of Fig. S4(a), which corresponds to the surface oxidation of the catalyst. Interestingly, the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid in 1.0 M ethanol in 1.0 M KOH solution exhibits an increasingly sharp anodic peak at an onset potential of 0.45 V vs. SCE for the EOR. However, the EOR peak current density of 37.45 mA cm−2 suggests that the EOR occurs at 0.72 V vs. SCE. Moreover, another oxidation signal during reverse scan at an applied potential of 0.66 V vs. SCE could be due to the oxidation of adsorbed intermediates during the anodic cycle. The possible intermediates are CO, CH3CHO and CH3COOH. Oxidation of these intermediates during the cathodic segment thus confirms the oxidation of ethanol to CO2 over the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid. The presence of Cu–C on the electrode surface has a significant effect in increasing the current density through the reaction of ethanol electro-oxidation. The oxidation of ethanol, as described, is generally confronted with the poisoning of the electrode. It has been reported in many studies in the literature that this is probably due to the contamination of the surface by intermediate species formed during the oxidation of ethanol. CO tolerance of the catalyst can be calculated from the ratio of forward oxidation peak current density (jf) to the reverse oxidation current density (jb). From the CV data of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid, the CO poisoning ratio (jf/jb) for the ethanol oxidation reaction is found to be 1.73. To assess the electrocatalytic performance of the synthesized material, we compared its activity towards the EOR, with those of commercial Pt/C and several state-of-the-art non-precious metal based electrocatalysts reported in the literature. The synthesized material shows a significant shifting of onset potential to a more positive value, 0.52 V vs. SCE, and a lower current density of 20.3 mA cm−2 in 1.0 M KOH with 1.0 M ethanol, from this it is inferred that our Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid catalyst has a better response for ethanol oxidation when compared to Pt/C. When compared with other reported electrocatalysts, this material demonstrates higher onset and half wave potential. These findings confirm that although the catalytic activity is still at an early developmental stage, the material’s low-cost synthesis, good stability, and structural tunability may provide a useful platform for future optimization strategies. Further enhancement, such as heteroatom doping or incorporation into composite frameworks, could significantly improve its performance, aligning it more closely with high performance multi-metallic systems.

      The effect of the increasing scan rate on the electrooxidation of ethanol was investigated and is shown in Fig. S5(d). Accordingly, the superimposed cyclic voltammograms were recorded at scan rate values ranging from 10 to 100 mV s−1. Two distinct anodic peaks are observed in the forward sweep, which can be attributed to sequential oxidation processes. The first peak at lower potential is likely due to the oxidation of adsorbed intermediate species, while the second peak corresponds to the actual oxidation of the species i.e., ethanol in the bulk electrolyte. The appearance of these two peaks becomes more distinguishable at higher scan rates due to enhanced kinetic resolution as seen in Fig. S5(d). The dual anodic peaks observed for the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid arise from stepwise copper oxidation coupled with multistage ethanol oxidation in alkaline media. The lower-potential peak corresponds to the oxidation of Cu0 to Cu+ (Cu2O) and the initial adsorption and dehydrogenation of ethanol on Cu0/Cu+ interfacial sites. The higher-potential peak is associated with the further oxidation of Cu+ to Cu2+ species (CuOOH/Cu(OH)2), which facilitates the deeper oxidation of ethanol-derived intermediates. The scan-rate-dependent CV response (10–100 mV s−1) indicates that the former process is mainly surface-controlled, while the latter involves diffusion-influenced faradaic kinetics, explaining the appearance of two distinct anodic peaks.56,57  It has been observed that upon increasing the scan rate, the peak current significantly increases and the onset potential decreases slightly with increasing scan rate, which indicates kinetic limitations associated with mass transport or the charge transfer process at higher scan rates. At a faster scan rate, time is shorter to build the diffusion layer across the electrode, so the current associated with thin-layer diffusion increases more rapidly with scan rate compared to that governed by semi-infinite (bulk) diffusion, due to the limited diffusion path length in the thin-layer regime. From this, it has been concluded that this is a diffusion control process and represents the fast electron transfer kinetics at higher scan rates. Fig. S5(e) shows the corresponding linear fit for the increasing scan rate values, yielding a slope of 0.18 and an R-square value of 0.98.

      Mechanism of electrooxidation of ethanol on the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid

      Copper and cuprous oxide play a significant role in electrochemistry due to their excellent electrical conductivity, corrosion resistance and ability to undergo redox reactions due to their multiple oxidation states mainly Cu(0), Cu(i) and Cu(ii). Carbon-based materials used as supports play a crucial role due to their high electrical conductivity, chemical stability, and tunable surface properties. Transition metal oxides supported on carbon nanostructures have a significant effect on ethanol oxidation due to enhanced conductivity and electron transfer. The carbon backbone resists corrosion in electrochemical environments making it ideal for long term applications. The versatility of carbon in electrochemistry makes it indispensable in energy storage and conversion applications.

      Role of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid in ethanol oxidation

      The Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid efficiently catalyzes ethanol oxidation due to its redox active Cu species. The alkaline medium KOH enhances the reaction by providing OH− ions, stabilizing Cu(i) sites and accelerating oxidation. Electrochemical potential also has a significant effect on ethanol oxidation. At a lower potential (<0.15 V vs. SCE) ethanol adsorbs on Cu but oxidation is sluggish. In the moderate potential region (∼0.35–0.55 V vs. SCE), surface Cu(ii)–OH species are generated and facilitate ethanol oxidation by enhancing the adsorption and dehydrogenation of ethanol intermediates. At higher potentials (>0.756 V vs. SCE), further oxidation results in the formation of less conductive/passivating copper oxide layers (Cu2O/CuO), leading to diminished catalytic performance. The catalytic activity originates from the synergistic coexistence of Cu0 and Cu+ species, as neither metallic Cu nor Cu2O alone is sufficient for efficient ethanol oxidation. Excessive oxidation disrupts this optimal Cu0/Cu+ balance by blocking active sites and reducing conductivity, thereby lowering performance. The oxidation of ethanol on the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid electrode in an alkaline medium involves complex electrocatalytic steps, where both Cu(0) and Cu(i) play a crucial role in facilitating electron transfer and breaking molecular bonds.65,66  Copper acts as an active catalytic site for ethanol oxidation. The enhanced stability of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid arises from the in situ formed carbonaceous matrix. The carbon layer stabilizes Cu/Cu2O nanoparticles, provides high conductivity, and enhances the surface area, thereby providing more active sites. The ethanol oxidation reaction proceeds through the formation of adsorbed intermediates such as acetaldehyde, and acetate species. Under alkaline conditions, further oxidation of carbonaceous adsorbates may occur, producing CO2, which is subsequently converted into bicarbonates/carbonate species in the electrolyte.       Experimental

      Materials and methods

      Copper(ii) sulphate pentahydrate (CuSO4·5H2O, ≥99%), starch (1.2% solution), ascorbic acid (≥99%), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥98%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (USA). Absolute ethanol (99.99%) was obtained from Merck (Germany). All chemicals were of analytical grade and used without further purification. All solutions were prepared using ultrapure water (resistivity ≥18.2 MΩ cm) obtained from a Millipore Milli-Q purification system. This water is used as a solvent throughout the synthesis of the electrocatalysts, as well as for their electrochemical and electrocatalytic studies.

      Synthesis of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid

      The Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid was synthesised using copper(ii) sulphate pentahydrate (CuSO4·5H2O) as the metal precursor, while ascorbic acid served as both the reducing and capping agent for Cu nanoparticle synthesis. In a typical synthesis, 0.1 M CuSO4·5H2O was dissolved in 20 mL deionized water and stirred for 15 min, followed by the addition of 100 mL of freshly prepared starch solution under continuous stirring at 80 °C. Subsequently, 50 mL of 0.2 M ascorbic acid was introduced, reducing Cu2+ ions to metallic Cu0 and initiating nanoparticle nucleation. The slow, dropwise addition of 30 mL of 1 M NaOH created a mildly alkaline environment, inducing partial surface oxidation of Cu0 to Cu2O. Simultaneously, starch underwent alkaline-assisted partial carbonisation, forming a carbonaceous matrix that stabilised and anchored the Cu/Cu2O nanoparticles, thereby yielding the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid. The observed greenish-yellow colour changes during the reaction confirmed the progressive reduction of Cu2+ species and nanohybrid formation. Uniform nanohybrid formation was ensured by controlled reduction kinetics, regulated NaOH addition, the soft-templating and steric stabilisation effects of starch, and a constant reflux temperature of 80 °C, maintained for 2 h based on preliminary optimisation studies aimed at achieving homogeneous Cu/Cu2O–C formation and optimal catalytic activity. After completion, the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature, and the precipitate was filtered, thoroughly washed with deionized water and ethanol, and dried at 60 °C for 12 h. The overall synthesis process is schematically illustrated in Scheme 1, and the obtained nanohybrid was stored in a glass vial for further characterisation and electrochemical studies.

      Catalyst characterization

      The crystal structure of the synthesized catalyst was determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using an XRD-6000 (Bruker D2 PHASER) operating at 40 kV and 40 mA. The measurements were performed in steps of 0.02° (2θ) at room temperature. The microstructure of the catalyst was investigated by scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) by using Carl Zeiss model Supra 55 from Germany. To determine the particle size, shape and size distribution, TEM analysis was carried out using JEOL JEM 2100 PLUS. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed using Omicron ESCA made by Oxford Instrument, Germany. FTIR spectroscopy was performed using a Shimadzu spectrophotometer. The UV-vis absorption measurement was carried out at room temperature on a Shimadzu UV-vis spectrophotometer (model λ−25) using a quartz cell (1 cm path).

      Preparation of the modified electrode

      All electrochemical studies were performed using a CHI-660E potentiostat (CH-instrument, USA) with a three-electrode cell comprising saturated calomel (SCE), a Pt wire, and a glassy carbon electrode (GCE, 3 mm diameter), which were used as a reference electrode, a counter electrode, and a working electrode, respectively. These electrodes were obtained from CH-Instruments, USA. The glassy carbon electrode (GCE, 3 mm diameter) was polished with 1 µm, 0.3 µm, and 0.05 µm alumina (Al2O3) slurries on a polishing cloth, followed by sequential rinsing with acetone and ethanol, and again with ultrapure water to remove inorganic and organic impurities simultaneously. This procedure was performed prior to each experiment to ensure a reproducible and clean electrode surface. Each GCE was cleaned individually following the same procedure to ensure consistent surface preparation. The catalyst ink for the working electrode was prepared by dispersing 5 mg of the catalyst in a solvent containing a 180 : 20 µL mixture of isopropanol and Nafion (5 wt%) solution, followed by sonication for 60 min to achieve a homogenous ink. This ink was drop-cast onto the GCE surface and air dried at room temperature. The electrolyte for the EOR consisted of 1 M KOH and 1 M ethanol. The catalyst was activated by conducting cyclic voltammetry (CV) scans in N2 saturated 1.0 M KOH until a reproducible cyclic curve was obtained before any other electrochemical measurements.

      Conclusions

      In this work, a noble metal free Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid electrocatalytic system has been synthesized by a simple chemical reduction approach and characterized by techniques such as powder XRD, FE-SEM with EDAX, TEM, HR-TEM and XPS. A remarkable EOR performance was demonstrated by this catalyst. The electrochemical investigation of the Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid shows better electrochemical performance in the presence of ethanol. The catalyst exhibits a lower onset potential of 0.45 V vs. SCE and achieves the highest current density of 37.45 mA cm−2 in 1.0 M KOH containing 1.0 M ethanol. Moreover, it shows superior stability up to 10 000 s for the EOR. Thus, the synthesized Cu/Cu2O–C nanohybrid exhibits potential for application in electrooxidation of ethanol in fuel cells and provides a basis for the future research direction related to ethanol oxidation on copper carbon nanohybrids. This report provides a new avenue for the utilization of combination of transition metals (Cu) along with carbon and investigates their electrochemical performance towards the production of renewable energy sources.
